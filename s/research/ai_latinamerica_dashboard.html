How much can AI add to GDP in Latin America?

Acemoglu's (2025) accounting with public data for 22 countries of Latin America and the Caribbean: TFP = exposure × profitability × cost saving × labor share, GDP = TFP × a capital multiplier. The benchmark uses Aghion and Bunel's (2024) calibration: AI costs falling 22% a year for ten years and a 40% cost saving; Acemoglu's own calibration (2023 costs, 27%) is one of the scenarios. Move the inputs and the numbers recompute. Part (b) adds Baumol's structural change: the same AI wave evaluated with the sectoral weights that observed productivity trends produce in 10, 20 or 30 years.

Part (a), the accounting. Acemoglu (2025, Economic Policy), equations 14 and 15: TFP gain over ten years = GDP share of the wage bill in exposed tasks (e, from each country's employment by occupation in ILOSTAT and the task exposure of Eloundou et al. 2024) × share of those tasks worth automating (q, read on the Svanberg et al. 2024 cost curve at each country's labor-cost ratio m) × cost saving a × labor income share. Benchmark calibration, following Aghion and Bunel (2024): the AI system gets 22% cheaper every year (the trend in computing cost, Besiroglu and Hobbhahn 2022), so after ten years the curve is read at twelve times m and anchored at their 50% for the US; a = 40%, the mean of three experiments (Peng et al., Noy and Zhang, Brynjolfsson et al.). Acemoglu's calibration: q at 2023 costs (23% in the US) and a = 27%, or 7% on hard-to-learn tasks. Exposure is each country's own in every scenario (Aghion and Bunel use 60% for the US). Labor income share × labor income share (sL, ILOSTAT). GDP = TFP / sL when capital keeps a constant capital-output ratio; as a robustness check, with a CES technology between capital and labor of elasticity σKL = 0.7 (Oberfield and Raval 2021) the local multiplier is 1 + σKL sK/sL: with complements, capital responds less to a TFP gain. The numbers are level effects cumulated over ten years, not annual growth rates; divide by ten for the growth-rate equivalent.

Part (b), structural change. A Huneeus-Rogerson (2024) model with the nonhomothetic CES of Comin, Lashkari and Mestieri (2021) across eleven sectors (σ = 0.35) and Cobb-Douglas production in capital and labor (CES as robustness), run on each country's observed sectoral productivity trends (CEPALSTAT value added at constant prices, ILOSTAT employment, 2013-2025). The drift factor is the AI gain recomputed with the sectoral GDP weights of 2032, 2042 or 2052, relative to 2022 weights. It is available for thirteen countries; the others keep the 2022 weights.

Comparisons. Aghion and Bunel's (2024) own US number uses q = 50%, a = 40% and e = 60% (0.68 pp of TFP a year). Model GDP columns are solved in the model for the benchmark and for Acemoglu's calibration, and rescaled to first order for the other scenarios and for your own inputs. The IT revolution added about 1.5 pp a year to US output per hour in 1995-2004 (Byrne, Oliner and Sichel 2013) and IT investment about 0.3 pp a year to Latin America's GDP growth (Jorgenson and Vu 2007).

Scale effects (extension). The bound is about existing tasks. The switch adds one channel it leaves out: if AI makes research tasks cheaper like any other task, the frontier produces more ideas. In a semi-endogenous growth model (Jones 1995; Aghion, Jones and Jones 2019) with ideas getting harder to find at β = 3.1 (Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb 2020, Table 7), research input rises by −ln(1 − e × q × a) = 4.1% at the frontier and TFP ends 1.3% higher in the long run; with adoption phased in over ten years, 0.18% has arrived by year ten. The same TFP gain is added to every country (frontier ideas are assumed to reach all of them, an upper bound) and GDP = TFP × the capital multiplier. It does not depend on the sliders.

Input-output links. Following Filippucci, Gal and Schief (2024) with OECD TiVA 2025, the bound splits into a direct effect (sectoral gains in gross-output terms, value-added weights) and an input-output multiplier (Domar weights). The two add up to the bound, so the last table column shows how much of it arrives inside the adopting sectors; the rest travels through cheaper inputs. TiVA covers Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Mexico and Peru.

Caveats. Exposure uses US task scores applied to each country's occupational mix at 1 or 2 digits of ISCO; earnings are monthly, not hourly; employer surcharges are statutory; the labor share imputes self-employment income. Everything here is an upper bound on the ten-year effect through existing tasks: new tasks and products, general-equilibrium price responses and effects beyond ten years are outside it.
